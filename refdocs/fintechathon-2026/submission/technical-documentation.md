# PaySense — technical documentation (draft for the submission)

> FinTechathon, Topic A. Every figure below is copied from a generated report and names it; re-copy after any re-run. The security design is summarised here and given in full in [`SECURITY.md`](../../../SECURITY.md).

## Context for readers outside Malaysia
Amounts are in Malaysian ringgit (RM; a ringgit is a hundred sen, and PaySense counts in sen). SPayLater (Shopee), TikTok PayLater and Atome are the buy-now-pay-later (BNPL) services students meet most at online checkouts; each splits a purchase into instalments, sometimes with a fee, up to a limit the provider sets. Many students' income arrives in lumps: PTPTN (the national higher-education loan fund) pays once a semester; allowances from family or a scholarship arrive monthly. Small instalments make a phone feel affordable today; several at once can outrun an income that arrives in lumps.

## 1. What PaySense does
A student signs in, brings in their history (file import, manual entry, or by pasting text to the assistant), and sees where their money is heading. Before a BNPL purchase they ask "can I afford it?" — in a form or in plain words to the assistant — and get Safe / Caution / At Risk, the reasons, and the smallest change that would make it Safe, or an honest "none does". Beside it, the **Second-Opinion Check** shows what this one purchase adds to their chance of running short, every instalment they already owe across providers month by month, and when the purchase would add little (§3.4). Budgets, bill collisions, spending insights and ranked recommendations come from rules over their own data. Nothing changes until they press Confirm.

## 2. Architecture
Next.js frontend → FastAPI backend (the only process with database access, service key server-side) → Supabase Postgres (Singapore, row-level security on, no anonymous policies). The two engines run inside the backend from committed ONNX artefacts; training is offline (`backend/ml/`) and the backend never imports it. The assistant layer calls an LLM provider (Gemini now, Claude later, one interface) with the student's message and the figures the tools computed. Diagram: [`README.md`](../../../README.md#architecture).

## 3. Core algorithms

### 3.1 Financial health forecast (Engine 1)
- Known flows (confirmed recurring income and bills, BNPL instalments) are placed on their dates; only the variable part — weekly discretionary spending and unconfirmed income — is forecast.
- Candidates fixed by the literature review (ARIMA, Prophet, LSTM) were compared with benchmarks (naive, 4- and 13-week moving averages) on two public datasets and a synthetic student cohort, by rolling-origin evaluation and a pre-registered rule. **Served: a global LSTM** — geometric-mean MASE **0.8153** against **1.0438** for the best benchmark (13-week moving average); median per-series difference −0.1095, 95% CI [−0.1504, −0.0779], Holm-adjusted Wilcoxon p = 2.4e−37 over 242 series (`reports/engine1/README.md`).
- 1,000 simulated futures from the forecast and recent forecast errors give the running margin's 80% band, the chance of running short, and a health score. Errors come from the student's own record, or below 26 weeks of history from a population record scaled to the student's level (D-61); spending and income are floored at zero per simulated month (D-60); the widening is fitted by history length. On 500 never-used synthetic students the band holds the 13-week total **0.80** of the time for spending and **0.81** for income (target 0.80; the previous rule 0.71 / 0.52), and against what happened in held-back weeks of the simulated ledgers the chance of running short scores Brier **0.0182** / **0.0309** (13 / 26-week cuts) against 0.0470 / 0.1182 for a constant (`reports/engine1/band_v3.md`, `reports/engine1/outcome_check.md`). Two limits belong beside those figures: the pooled gain in Brier comes from histories of 17-25 weeks, and for 26 weeks or more the previous rule scored lower (0.0175 against 0.0196 at 52+ weeks); and on real accounts (Berka, MoneyData; not students) the band holds the 13-week total less often than on the synthetic students, Berka spending at 17-25 weeks only 16% of the time (`reports/engine1/serving_check.md` section 3). A savings buffer the student enters is the starting point (D-50); otherwise RM 0.

### 3.2 BNPL risk check (Engine 2)
- The provider's own rules build the exact schedule. The label for training is Engine 1's simulated shortfall probability with the plan added (D-07): 11,000 synthetic students, 17 money-only features defined in one file (`features.py`), never age, gender, ethnicity, nationality or employment.
- **Two parts (D-62):** the chance of running short without the purchase comes straight from the simulation; the model learns only the share the purchase adds, and the served chance is the two combined. Candidates: random forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, with logistic regression, a constant and "no added risk" (the simulated chance without the purchase) as benchmarks; 5×5 cross-validation, tuning, calibration chosen on out-of-fold predictions, a new lockbox of 1,000 students scored once. **Served: LightGBM with Platt calibration** — lockbox Brier **0.0078** against 0.0126 for logistic regression, 0.0532 for "no added risk" and 0.1608 for the constant; Brier skill 0.951; the same band as the direct simulation for 95.5% of fresh students (`reports/engine2/versions.md`, `serving_check.md`).
- Reasons are exact Shapley values of the purchase's added share, so they explain how much the purchase adds; they can include features of the student's starting position (for example a margin that was already low), because the added share depends on them. When the student was short before buying, the screen says nothing about the purchase stands out (56.6% of non-Safe results) and the Second-Opinion block shows why. The counterfactual search tries fewer or more instalments, waiting, a lower price and another provider, and reports only a change the model itself rates Safe.

### 3.3 The assistant
- **Three tiers (D-48):** Tier 0 reads the student's data, Tier 1 computes or stores a *pending* proposal, Tier 2 writes — and Tier 2 is not a tool the model can call: it runs only from the confirm endpoint after the student presses Confirm on a card built from the stored proposal (digest, expiry, single-use claim).
- **No figure of its own (D-47):** every number in the model's answer must come from this turn's tool results, the server's step summaries or the student's own message (figures the model wrote into its own tool arguments do not count); otherwise the answer is replaced by the tools' summary.
- **Untrusted data:** transaction descriptions and pasted text reach the model as labelled data; an instruction inside them can at most lead to a proposal the student sees. Pasted rows must each match one line of the paste.
- **Caps:** six tool calls and a deadline per turn, per-user and global daily token budgets, a per-user request quota, one turn at a time. If the provider fails, guided mode keeps every tool usable without a model.
- **Measured:** live evaluation on the deployment model, Anthropic `claude-haiku-5-5`, 13 scenarios x 3 runs (39 turns): **39 / 39** tasks completed, 0 guided fallbacks, 3 answers replaced by the number check, latency p50 4.1 s and p95 6.3 s (`reports/assistant/README.md`). It reached that in three steps, each with its acceptance rule written before the re-run (`refdocs/plans/2026-10-19-assistant-follow-up.md`): the first run scored 34 / 39, because "Go ahead with that." straight after a check failed (0 / 3, also 0 / 2 on `claude-sonnet-5-5`) and an under-specified question failed twice; the causes were a missing prompt rule (rule 9), a number check that treated a price the student typed earlier, and today's date, as invented, and replaced correct answers with a template. One intermediate run (36 / 39) failed its own rule as written and is recorded as failed. The sample is small (three runs per scenario). The earlier evaluation on the free-tier `gemini-3.1-flash-lite` completed 36 / 36 on 12 scenarios (`reports/assistant/gemini-3.1-flash-lite/README.md`); different model and scenario set. The demo story was also run in a browser against the live model (`evidence/live-browser/`). The demo story run end to end through the API: 20 requests, 0 failed, plan, budget and paste confirmed, an injected instruction ignored (`evidence/demo-run/SUMMARY.md`).

### 3.4 The Second-Opinion Check (D-57)
Malaysia's Consumer Credit Act makes lenders assess a borrower's total commitments, but each BNPL provider sees only its own plans and no one shows the student the whole picture. On every risk check PaySense shows:
- the simulated chance of running short **without** the purchase, **with** it, and the **points it adds** — all from one Engine 1 simulation with one seed, the quantity Engine 2's label is built from (D-07);
- every unpaid instalment across every provider, **stacked by month** with the new plan and the confirmed income falling in that month;
- the exposure before and after (unpaid total, providers, plans) and, where the student recorded their limits, whether those limits together reach RM 1,500, the level at which Bank Negara Malaysia asks banks for a full affordability assessment (shown as information, never as compliance);
- **Find a better time:** start dates (as typed, or the day after each of the next two confirmed income dates) × the instalment counts the provider offers, each judged by the same simulation; the first that adds at most 5 points and stays below the At Risk edge is offered with "Use this", which re-runs the check (ASSUMED 43). A student already At Risk without the purchase is told that no timing fixes it.

The assistant reaches the same numbers through `check_purchase_risk` and a Tier 1 tool `find_safer_timing`; a saved report keeps the second opinion the student decided on. Figures on the demo personas: `demo-scenario.md` (columns *Adds* and *Safer timing*).

## 4. Security design (summary)
Signed bearer tokens with revocation; a passphrase second factor (scrypt); every query scoped to the signed-in user in the repository layer and by composite foreign keys; RLS with no anonymous access; nonce CSP; body limits, rate limits, daily quotas and a ledger cap; artefact checks at start-up; money as integer sen; the assistant's tiers, confirm and number check; an audit log of every assistant step; consent, export and delete for the student's data. An independent review found no critical or high issues; its medium and low findings were fixed with tests or listed as known risks. Scans: 0 known vulnerabilities in the backend's dependencies and the frontend's production dependencies; 0 secrets in the git history (`evidence/scans/SUMMARY.md`). Full controls table, threat model and 15 known risks: [`SECURITY.md`](../../../SECURITY.md).

**On screen: the security drill (D-58).** The `/security` page runs the four named attack classes — induced transfers, data extraction, privilege escalation, prompt injection — through the real assistant code, each attack in its own throwaway sandbox, with a scripted model that obeys every injected instruction. Result: **14 attacks, the model fooled in 14, harm done in 0** (`evidence/security-drill/SUMMARY.md`). The tests switch controls off to prove a broken control shows up as harm.

Use the paragraph in [`honest-limits.md`](../honest-limits.md) verbatim.
